At Brendi, a food-delivery platform for restaurants, I own an AI agent that manages paid traffic for hundreds of stores. It creates ads, kills bad ones, moves budget. Every week, on its own, with real money. I call it Anderson.

The problem with an agent like this is easy to state and hard to solve: **how do you change it without breaking it?** An idea that looks good on paper can make hundreds of restaurants worse off at the same time. And "it seems better" is not an acceptable answer when the money belongs to someone else.

The answer we settled on: never change everything at once. Every new version of Anderson runs against the current one, in half the stores, for two weeks. Only what wins becomes the baseline. This post is about that structure, what it has produced so far, and what it has already taught me.

---

## The structure

A test cycle has six steps. Always the same ones.

<CicloDeTeste lang="en" />

A few details that matter:

- **A version is one hypothesis.** Not a bundle of improvements. One change, behind one code flag. If the version wins, I know why.
- **The draw is stratified by store size.** Stores are wildly different; some make 100× what others do. So they are split into four bands by revenue over the previous 13 days, and the draw happens inside each band. Then it checks itself: if the two groups' pre-test averages differ by more than 2%, it redraws, up to 20 times. If it still can't close the gap, the test doesn't start.
- **The roster is frozen.** Who is in which group is written down on day zero and never changes.
- **The read compares each group with itself.** The dashboard shows how much each group sold before and how much it sells during, per store and per band. What matters is the difference between the two growth rates, not the raw gap between the groups.
- **The decision is binary.** Graduate to 100% or discard. The next version is born testing against the new baseline.

## The rhythm

Since the structure exists, a verdict comes out every two weeks. So far:

| Version | What it tested | Result | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.2 | Creative variety and budget protection: the agent stops repeating the same product and stops spending on ads that don't deliver | Graduated | Became the baseline |
| 1.3 | A playbook of paid-traffic strategies the agent consults | Dashboard said +19% revenue per store; after discounting the unbalanced draw, about +7% | Graduated (Sep 2026) |
| 1.4 | The signal used to kill bad ads: from pixel purchases to cost per initiated checkout | Not worse, direction positive, not proven | Graduated as the new baseline (Sep 14 to Sep 25, 2026) |
| 1.5 | Product deep link: new ads send the click to the product's page instead of the menu root | Running since Sep 27, 2026 | Read due mid-October |

1.4 deserves one more line. The old signal, "how many purchases the pixel attributed to this ad", is 0, 1 or 2 for most ads. Too small to tell a bad ad from an unlucky one. In small stores, half the ads were being decided on zero information. Cost per initiated checkout predicts real orders better than the pixel purchase, and goes blind 7× less often, which leaves fewer small stores in the dark. The clean window of the test was seven days, with 24 real orders against 53. The honest interval runs from −27% to +91%. That doesn't prove it's better. It proves it isn't worse, while killing about 2× more bad ads. By the criterion set before looking at the data ("just don't lose"), it graduated.

1.5 too. It was born testing two things: product deep links and custom audiences. On the first run, Meta blocked audience writes for the whole fleet, over a terms-acceptance step that has no API. Instead of cancelling the test, the hypothesis was narrowed: deep link only. The draw and the roster stayed the same.

## One cycle from the inside: 1.3 and the crooked draw

Today's structure wasn't born finished. It was born from a mistake in 1.3.

After two weeks of playbook, the dashboard showed the new group making **19% more** revenue per store. It looked settled. Before signing off, I asked the cheapest question in an A/B test, the one almost nobody asks: **were the two groups alike before I changed anything?**

I took the 13 days before the start and compared. The new group **was already selling 9% more**. It wasn't one giant store skewing the average; the median showed the same gap. The draw, which back then was a coin flip per store, had simply come out crooked. With stores this different in size, a 9% gap by bad luck is common.

It's like comparing two classes after a new lesson: class A got higher grades. But class A already had higher grades before the lesson. Part of the edge isn't the lesson. It's the class.

Adjusting for store size, the edge I was measuring became **7%**. That is the real gain.

The playbook worked. Ads got 3× better at the median, and the crooked draw doesn't explain that. But the number on screen was more than double the honest one. And the number on screen is what becomes the team's memory, the plan, the budget.

On 1.4, the new draw ran for real for the first time. It rejected the first attempt. The second settled at a **−0.86%** gap between groups, versus the +9% the previous time.

## What the structure has already taught us

- **Hypotheses come from data, not hunches.** 1.4 came out of a research question: which signal an ad generates best predicts a delivered order? The answer became the version. Without the structure, the idea would have gone to the whole fleet in an ordinary deploy.
- **You can narrow without cancelling.** When Meta blocked audiences midway through 1.5, the test went on with a smaller hypothesis. The cycle didn't reset to zero.
- **Measure delivered orders, not clicks.** Per store, per size band, against each group's own pre-test period. A single number hides: 1.3's gain came almost entirely from one segment, and was negative in the smallest stores.
- **Cheap experiments keep the rhythm.** Each new version costs one code flag and one row in a table. That is why the two-week cadence holds.

## What I would change next

Two things.

The first is coverage. 1.5's deep link only applies to ads created from a product, about a quarter of new ads. Half the stores see the new version, but only a fraction of their ads actually change. Before starting a test, I want to know how many ads the hypothesis reaches, and if it's few, widen the hypothesis or accept that the read will take longer.

The second is reading every test with the same ruler. I read 1.3 one way and 1.4 another, adjusting the ruler as I found problems. From now on, the reading rule is written before the draw and is the same for every version. A structure is only worth something if it is more rigid than my optimism.
